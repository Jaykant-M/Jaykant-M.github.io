A design-thinking approach, not a model-first one

The hard part of this project wasn't the maths - it was resisting the fancier model in favour of something a person could actually act on. That's a design problem, so I ran it as one: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test - as a loop, not a line.

Two users, not one
The CRM operator who has to act on a segment, and the customer who receives the treatment. Understood from behaviour, not surveys.

01
Empathise · The Problem
One Customer, Too Many Definitions

The starting point for this project was a familiar kind of mess: a CRM strategy running on multiple, overlapping definitions of who counts as an engaged customer - each one built for a different campaign, by a different team, at a different time. No single source of truth existed for the one decision that mattered most: what treatment does this customer get, right now?

The goal was to replace that patchwork with one actionable segmentation - a system that assigns every customer to exactly one segment, from the earliest point in their lifecycle, and gets more confident about that assignment as real behaviour accumulates rather than waiting for it.

A customer should receive one clear CRM treatment from day one, with the segmentation evolving automatically as more behavioural evidence arrives - not the other way around.

That constraint - usable from day one, not just eventually accurate - shaped almost every methodological choice that followed.

02
Empathise · Data Understanding
Reading the Data Before Modelling It

Before building anything, the dataset needed a proper interrogation - not just a null-count and a shrug. A few patterns turned out to matter a lot more than they first appeared to:

  • Structural nulls aren't missing data. A blank "days since payment method added" field wasn't a data gap to impute - it meant the customer had never registered a payment method at all. Imputing a value there would have manufactured a signal that didn't exist. It stayed in as a categorical flag instead.
  • Some fields were logically impossible, and that's useful. A subset of customers showed a route-repetition score higher than either of its own component scores - a mathematical impossibility given how the fields were defined. That's not an insight; it's a pipeline defect, and worth flagging to the data owner rather than modelling around.
  • The mean lies when a market has whales. A handful of extreme values pulled the average value per customer well above what a typical customer actually looks like. Comparing the maximum against a high percentile, rather than trusting the mean at face value, exposed how concentrated that risk was.
  • Outcome variables need to stay outcome variables. Value, maturity, and churn were kept strictly separate from the behavioural fields used to predict them throughout - mixing the two is the fastest way to build a segmentation that's secretly just circular.

The base itself was heavily front-loaded: a large share of customers churned out after a single interaction, and only a minority ever reached the point where "lifetime behaviour" was a meaningful concept. That single fact - most of the base never accumulates much history - became the central design problem for everything downstream.

03
Define · Differentiation
What Actually Drives Long-Term Value

Before building a segmentation, it's worth checking what actually separates a long-term, high-value customer from one who activates fast and disappears just as quickly. Comparing the two groups - plus the much larger group that never builds a habit at all - surfaced one dominant pattern and several genuinely counterintuitive ones.

Long-term value is created by habit formation, not by isolated high-intent transactions.

Customers who develop a stable, repeatable usage pattern go on to generate dramatically more long-run value than customers who show up with obvious purchase intent - a pre-booked, high-context, "clearly a real need" interaction - but never come back. High intent looks valuable in the moment. Routine is what actually pays off.

Vouchers ≠ price sensitivity

The highest-value, longest-retained customers redeemed the most vouchers - but relied the least on discounted service tiers. The group that never built a habit showed the opposite: light voucher use, heavy reliance on the cheapest available tier. The implication: voucher redemption reinforces already-valuable customers, while a structural preference for the discounted tier is the real price-sensitivity signal. Conflating the two into one "discount-seeker" segment would have hidden both findings.

The starting point matters more than the destination

Across every segment, repetition at the point where a customer starts an interaction was a stronger signal than repetition at where they end it. The practical implication is a targeting one: build routine-forming nudges around a customer's starting context, not their destination.

Satisfaction isn't a churn predictor here

The group that never built a habit rated their experience the most positively and rated it most often. The long-term, high-value group rated the least. Sentiment and retention pointed in opposite directions - which meant behaviour, not stated satisfaction, had to carry the segmentation.

Timing patterns don't differentiate

Rush-hour share and weekend share were nearly identical across every segment. Whatever separates a long-term customer from a one-off, it isn't when they show up.

04
Ideate → Prototype · Method
Building the Decision Hierarchy

With the drivers understood, the next question was purely methodological: how do you turn "routine formation matters most" into a segmentation every customer can be run through, that a non-technical stakeholder can actually explain?

The answer was an ordered rule hierarchy - ranked threshold rules evaluated top to bottom, first match wins, output is a plain IF/THEN/ELSE. Every customer lands in exactly one segment, and every assignment has a rule behind it that a stakeholder can read and challenge.

1. Design constraintDefine

One customer, one segment. No leftover bucket. Actionable from the earliest point possible.

2. Method, by eliminationIdeate

Four more obvious approaches were ruled out first - clustering, RFM, value deciles, and persona flags (shown below) - which is what left an ordered rule hierarchy.

3. Admissible variablesPrototype

Routine formation, commercial intent (business or premium usage), discount dependence, geographic stability, and planned/high-intent demand. Everything else - acquisition metadata, ratings, redundant fields - excluded by design.

4. Let the data decide the rankingPrototype

A shallow decision tree, run on behavioural variables only, was used purely to rank the admissible variables by importance - not to set thresholds. Routine formation dominated everything else combined.

5. The ladderPrototype

Routine + commercial intent, then routine alone, then discount dependence, then geographic-only activity, then planned/high-intent demand, with everything else falling to a final catch-all. Discount dependence is deliberately ranked above the geography and intent rules - it's a spending decision that can show up anywhere in the funnel, so it needs to be caught wherever it appears.

6. ValidationTest

Differences between segments were cross-checked against actual value and maturity outcomes, not just behavioural description - and the rule set was tested on a held-out slice of new customers to confirm it generalised rather than overfitting the data it was built on.

That single comparison did more to justify the segmentation than any behavioural description could.

05
Prototype · Application
CRM Strategy by Segment

A segmentation is only as good as the decisions it changes. Each segment was mapped to a distinct lifecycle treatment - and, just as importantly, to a short list of what not to do:

  • Premium Habit Builders - retain, expand, cross-sell into higher-tier and business usage through account-management channels. Deliberately avoid discounting: this segment already shows the lowest discount reliance of any group, and a promotion here would be pure margin giveaway.
  • Habit Builders - protect the routine with light nudges only when a break in pattern is detected. Avoid aggressive discounting: the data shows no measurable uplift from pushing promotions into an already-loyal segment.
  • Promotion Dependent - withdraw the subsidy. Remove from promotional eligibility, shift to organic-only communication, and monitor for exploiter behaviour (multi-account creation, offer stacking).
  • Explorers - serve, don't nurture. This segment rides exclusively outside its home market, so domestic retention campaigns are wasted spend; the right moment is around travel and destination context instead.
  • Occasional Utility - convert the occasion into a routine with a post-interaction prompt to save the frequent context (a location, a route) as a favourite. Avoid mistaking high-intent volume for high value - it isn't the same thing.
  • Trialists - one time-boxed activation nudge after the first interaction, then stop. This segment shows zero routine, zero pre-booking, and zero high-intent signal - an extended nurture sequence has nothing to build on yet.
06
Prototype · Automation
Solving Cold Start: A Progressive Signal Framework

The rule hierarchy above has one obvious hole: it needs behavioural data to run, and a genuinely new customer has none yet. Waiting until enough interactions accumulate to run the real rules isn't a minor edge case - the earlier data-quality work had already shown that a large majority of the base never reaches that point at all. Waiting means never segmenting most of the base, and defaulting them to a generic, non-personalised treatment indefinitely.

Signup
Assign a provisional "cold-start tier" immediately, using only acquisition-based signals available at registration - referral, onboarding voucher, verification status, geography.
First interaction
The real behavioural signal begins the moment the first interaction completes - domestic vs. abroad, commercial vs. standard usage.
Subsequent interactions
Each new data point tightens the confidence behind the eventual segment, without changing the underlying rules that define it.

Behavioural segments stay fixed. What evolves is the strength of the evidence supporting each one.

The signup-based starting point isn't optional - it's mandatory, precisely because behavioural fields are ratios over completed interactions, and their precision is capped by how many interactions a customer has actually had. A customer can't be well-described by a ratio with almost no denominator. The provisional tier exists purely to give every customer some defensible treatment from minute one, which then gets replaced by real evidence as fast as that evidence arrives.

07
Prototype · Automation
Keeping Segments Honest: Promote Fast, Demote Slow

Once segments can shift automatically, a new failure mode appears: reacting to noise. One irregular interaction shouldn't flip a loyal customer's treatment overnight, but a genuine change in circumstance shouldn't sit unaddressed for weeks either. The system needed different reaction speeds for different kinds of evidence.

Positive Evidence
Negative Evidence
What?
Repeat pattern, emerging routine
One irregular interaction, pattern break
Now what?
Upgrade immediately

Why?
Evidence of habit is real and should be reinforced quickly
One irregular interaction doesn't mean the habit is lost

A third category - structural signals like deep discount dependence - sits outside this speed trade-off entirely: it's applied immediately and overrides the hierarchy, because a spending pattern like that is unlikely to fluctuate interaction-to-interaction the way a single missed routine day might.

The Automated CRM Decision Engine

Segment membership recalculates after every completed interaction - but recalculating isn't the same as acting. A three-tier alerting framework separates the two, so the system reacts to real change without firing on noise:

T1
Immediate response
A confirmed shift into a risk state or a broken routine triggers a real-time, customer-facing action - the tier that can't wait for the next cycle.
T2
Next-cycle journey
A segment confirmed after sufficient evidence moves the customer into the appropriate lifecycle journey on the next scheduled cycle - deliberate, not reactive.
T3
Track only
Minor, unconfirmed fluctuations are logged for trend purposes but generate no customer-facing communication at all - watched, not acted on.

A simple cooldown on how often a customer can be pulled into a newly triggered journey keeps the engine from causing the exact message fatigue it was built to prevent.

08
Test · Recommendations
What's Still Missing

The segmentation works with what's available today, but three information gaps stood out as the highest-value additions:

Timing signals
The gap between a customer's first and second interaction, and how that gap trends. Two customers can look identical after two interactions today, but returning after two days versus two months represents a completely different trajectory - and there's currently no clock on that.
Service friction
Cancellations, unfulfilled requests, and exposure to poor service conditions. Without this, low demand and failed demand look identical - there's no way to tell whether a customer left because they didn't want to come back, or because the service failed them first.
Customer intent
Signals from before the very first completed interaction - conversion from quote to booking, searches, sessions. This would allow segmentation to begin before any behavioural history exists at all.

Priority order matters here. Routine formation is the strongest predictor of long-term value uncovered in this analysis, but it only becomes measurable after multiple interactions. Timing and friction signals are what would let CRM catch a high-risk customer several interactions earlier than the current framework can - before the window to intervene has closed.

Process
The Repeatable Process Behind It

The specifics above are one application. The underlying approach is the part that transfers - design thinking applied to a data problem, run as a loop rather than a line. The same sequence carries to any behavioural-segmentation-for-lifecycle problem, whatever the dataset:

1
Frame
Define the one decision
Pin the segmentation to a single question CRM has to answer - what treatment does this customer get, and from when - before touching a variable.
2
Data understanding
Interrogate before modelling
Separate structural nulls from missing data, catch impossible values, distrust the mean where whales exist, and keep outcome fields out of the predictor set.
3
Differentiation
Isolate the real value drivers
Compare long-term vs. one-off customers to find what actually predicts value - and discard the intuitive signals that don't (here: satisfaction, timing).
4
Feature selection & ranking
Keep only admissible inputs, then rank them
Filter to behavioural, actionable, leak-free variables; use a shallow tree to order them by importance - not to set thresholds.
5
Modelling
Build an ordered rule hierarchy
Turn the ranking into first-match-wins IF/THEN rules: one customer, one segment, one readable reason a stakeholder can challenge.
6
Validation
Test against outcomes and a holdout
Cross-check segment differences against real value/maturity, and confirm the rules generalise on unseen new customers rather than overfitting.
7
Coverage
Solve cold start
Give brand-new customers a provisional tier from signup signals, then replace it with real evidence as behaviour arrives - so no one waits for treatment.
8
Activation & monitoring
Map to lifecycle actions, then self-correct
Attach each segment to one treatment and one "don't", recalculate after every interaction, and react at different speeds - promote fast, demote slow.
Loops back to step 2 continuously: every new interaction is fresh evidence.

Across all eight steps the same five design-thinking moves recur: empathise with the operator and the customer, define the one decision, ideate and eliminate, prototype the rules, then test and iterate.

09
Reflect · Synthesis
Closing Thought

Nothing in this framework required a more sophisticated model. The hard part - and the actual value delivered - was resisting the more elegant-looking options (clustering, personas, a black-box classifier) in favour of a segmentation a CRM stakeholder could read, trust, and act on immediately, with a clear answer to "why is this customer in this segment" every single time.

  • → An ordered, explainable rule beats a more accurate but opaque model, when the output has to survive a conversation with the person acting on it.
  • → Cold start isn't an edge case to patch later - for a base this front-loaded, it's the majority case, and it has to be designed for from day one.
  • → Reacting at different speeds to different kinds of evidence is what keeps an automated system from undermining its own trustworthiness through noise.

The best segment isn't the one that best describes a customer today. It's the one that tells CRM exactly what to do about them - and keeps telling the truth as more evidence comes in.
