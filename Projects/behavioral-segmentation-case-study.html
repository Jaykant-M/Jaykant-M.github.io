A design-thinking approach, not a model-first one

The hard part of behavioural segmentation is rarely the maths - it's resisting the fancier model in favour of something a person can actually act on. That makes it a design problem, so it's worth running as one: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test - as a loop, not a line.

01 Empathise
Two users, not one
The CRM operator who has to act on a segment, and the customer who receives the treatment. Understood from behaviour, not surveys.
02 Define
One decision to serve
What treatment does this customer get, and from when. Every later choice narrows to that single question.
03 Ideate
Options on the table
Clustering, RFM, value deciles, personas - four obvious approaches considered, and the reason each was ruled out.
04 Prototype
Build it to be read
An ordered, explainable rule hierarchy, a cold-start tier, and an automated decision engine - designed to be challenged.
05 Test
Prove it, then iterate
Validate against real value outcomes and a held-out slice of new customers, then stress-test for what it still can't see.
01
Empathise · The Problem
One Customer, Too Many Definitions

Most CRM teams will recognise the starting point: a strategy running on multiple, overlapping definitions of who counts as an engaged customer - each built for a different campaign, by a different team, at a different time. What's usually missing is a single source of truth for the one decision that matters most: what treatment does this customer get, right now?

The goal is to replace that patchwork with one actionable segmentation - a system that assigns every customer to exactly one segment from the earliest point in their lifecycle, and grows more confident in that assignment as real behaviour accumulates, rather than waiting for it.

A customer should receive one clear CRM treatment from day one, with the segmentation evolving automatically as more behavioural evidence arrives - not the other way around.

That constraint - usable from day one, not just eventually accurate - shapes almost every methodological choice that follows.

02
Empathise · Data Understanding
Four Data Traps to Check Before Modelling

Before building anything, the data deserves a proper interrogation - not just a null-count and a shrug. Four patterns tend to matter far more than they first appear to:

  • Structural nulls aren't missing data. In a ride-hailing app, a blank "days since payment method added" field usually isn't a gap to impute - it means the customer never registered a payment method. Imputing a value would manufacture a signal that doesn't exist; it belongs in the model as a categorical flag instead.
  • Logically impossible values are useful. If a combined score comes out higher than both of the components it's built from, that's not an insight - it's a pipeline defect, worth flagging to the data owner rather than modelling around.
  • The mean lies when a market has whales. A handful of extreme customers can pull the average value per customer far above what a typical customer looks like. Comparing the maximum against a high percentile, rather than trusting the mean, shows how concentrated that risk really is.
  • Outcome variables need to stay outcome variables. Value, maturity and churn should be kept strictly separate from the behavioural fields used to predict them - mixing the two is the fastest way to build a segmentation that's secretly circular.

Many consumer bases are also heavily front-loaded: a large share of customers leave after a single interaction, and only a minority ever reach the point where "lifetime behaviour" means anything. Where that's true - most customers never accumulate much history - it becomes the central design problem for everything downstream.

03
Define · Differentiation
Signals Worth Testing Before You Segment

Before building a segmentation, check what actually separates a long-term, high-value customer from one who activates fast and disappears just as quickly. Comparing the two groups - plus the usually much larger group that never builds a habit at all - tends to surface one dominant pattern and several counterintuitive ones. Treat the ones below as hypotheses to test on your own data, not universal facts.

Long-term value is usually created by habit formation, not by isolated high-intent transactions.

Customers who develop a stable, repeatable usage pattern typically generate far more long-run value than customers who arrive with obvious purchase intent - a pre-booked, high-context, "clearly a real need" interaction - but never come back. High intent looks valuable in the moment; routine is what tends to pay off.

Vouchers ≠ price sensitivity

Voucher redemption and price sensitivity are easy to conflate. Loyal, valuable customers can redeem plenty of vouchers while rarely choosing the cheapest service tier, and customers who never build a habit can show the opposite pattern. If so, vouchers are reinforcing already-valuable customers, while a structural preference for the discounted tier is the real price-sensitivity signal. Folding both into one "discount-seeker" segment would hide both.

Where customers start can matter more than where they go

Repetition in where a customer starts an interaction - a regular pickup point, for example - can be a stronger habit signal than repetition in where they end it. If that holds in your data, build routine-forming nudges around a customer's starting context, not their destination.

Satisfaction may not predict churn

It's tempting to assume happy customers stay. But customers who never build a habit can rate their experience the most positively and the most often, while long-term, high-value customers rate the least. When sentiment and retention point in different directions, behaviour - not stated satisfaction - has to carry the segmentation.

Timing patterns rarely differentiate

Rush-hour share and weekend share often look much the same across very different customers. Check before assuming: whatever separates a long-term customer from a one-off frequently isn't when they show up.

04
Ideate → Prototype · Method
Building the Decision Hierarchy

Once the drivers are understood, the next question is purely methodological: how do you turn "routine formation matters most" into a segmentation every customer can be run through - and that a non-technical stakeholder can actually explain?

The answer proposed here is an ordered rule hierarchy - ranked threshold rules evaluated top to bottom, first match wins, output is a plain IF/THEN/ELSE. Every customer lands in exactly one segment, and every assignment has a rule behind it that a stakeholder can read and challenge.

1. Design constraintDefine

One customer, one segment. No leftover bucket. Actionable from the earliest point possible.

2. Method, by eliminationIdeate

Rule out the more obvious approaches first - clustering, RFM, value deciles and persona flags (shown below). What's left standing is an ordered rule hierarchy.

3. Admissible variablesPrototype

In the ride-hailing example: routine formation, commercial intent (business or premium usage), discount dependence, geographic stability, and planned/high-intent demand. Everything else - acquisition metadata, ratings, redundant fields - is excluded by design.

4. Let the data decide the rankingPrototype

Fit a shallow decision tree on behavioural variables only, and use it purely to rank the admissible variables by importance - not to set thresholds. In a habit-driven business, expect routine formation to dominate.

5. The ladderPrototype

In the example, the ladder reads: routine + commercial intent, then routine alone, then discount dependence, then geographic-only activity, then planned/high-intent demand, with everything else falling to a final catch-all. Discount dependence is deliberately ranked above the geography and intent rules - it's a spending decision that can show up anywhere in the funnel, so it needs to be caught wherever it appears.

6. ValidationTest

Cross-check differences between segments against actual value and maturity outcomes, not just behavioural description - and test the rule set on a held-out slice of new customers to confirm it generalises rather than overfitting the data it was built on.

If so, that single comparison justifies the segmentation better than any behavioural description.

05
Prototype · Application
CRM Strategy by Segment

A segmentation is only as good as the decisions it changes. Each segment maps to a distinct lifecycle treatment - and, just as importantly, to a short list of what not to do:

  • Premium Habit Builders - retain, expand, cross-sell into higher-tier and business usage through account-management channels. Avoid discounting: customers who already rely least on discounts gain little from a promotion, and it gives away margin.
  • Habit Builders - protect the routine with light nudges only when a break in pattern is detected. Avoid aggressive discounting: pushing promotions into an already-loyal segment rarely produces measurable uplift.
  • Promotion Dependent - withdraw the subsidy. Remove from promotional eligibility, shift to organic-only communication, and monitor for exploiter behaviour (multi-account creation, offer stacking).
  • Explorers - serve, don't nurture. This segment is active mainly outside its home market, so domestic retention campaigns are wasted spend; the right moment is around travel and destination context instead.
  • Occasional Utility - convert the occasion into a routine with a post-interaction prompt to save the frequent context (a location, a route) as a favourite. Avoid mistaking high-intent volume for high value - it isn't the same thing.
  • Trialists - one time-boxed activation nudge after the first interaction, then stop. With no routine, no pre-booking and no high-intent signal yet, an extended nurture sequence has nothing to build on.
06
Prototype · Automation
Solving Cold Start: A Progressive Signal Framework

The rule hierarchy above has one obvious hole: it needs behavioural data to run, and a genuinely new customer has none yet. Waiting until enough interactions accumulate isn't a minor edge case - in a front-loaded base, most customers never reach that point. Waiting means never segmenting most of the base, and leaving them on a generic, non-personalised treatment indefinitely.

Signup
Assign a provisional "cold-start tier" immediately, using only acquisition-based signals available at registration - referral, onboarding voucher, verification status, geography.
First interaction
The real behavioural signal begins the moment the first interaction completes - domestic vs. abroad, commercial vs. standard usage.
Subsequent interactions
Each new data point tightens the confidence behind the eventual segment, without changing the underlying rules that define it.

Behavioural segments stay fixed. What evolves is the strength of the evidence supporting each one.

The signup-based starting point isn't optional - it's mandatory, precisely because behavioural fields are ratios over completed interactions, and their precision is capped by how many interactions a customer has actually had. A customer can't be well-described by a ratio with almost no denominator. The provisional tier exists purely to give every customer some defensible treatment from minute one, which then gets replaced by real evidence as fast as that evidence arrives.

07
Prototype · Automation
Keeping Segments Honest: Promote Fast, Demote Slow

Once segments can shift automatically, a new failure mode appears: reacting to noise. One irregular interaction shouldn't flip a loyal customer's treatment overnight, but a genuine change in circumstance shouldn't sit unaddressed for weeks either. The system needs different reaction speeds for different kinds of evidence.

Positive Evidence
Negative Evidence
What?
Repeat pattern, emerging routine
One irregular interaction, pattern break
Now what?

A third category - structural signals like deep discount dependence - sits outside this speed trade-off entirely: it's applied immediately and overrides the hierarchy, because a spending pattern like that is unlikely to fluctuate interaction-to-interaction the way a single missed routine day might.

The Automated CRM Decision Engine

Segment membership recalculates after every completed interaction - but recalculating isn't the same as acting. A three-tier alerting framework separates the two, so the system reacts to real change without firing on noise:

T1
Immediate response
A confirmed shift into a risk state or a broken routine triggers a real-time, customer-facing action - the tier that can't wait for the next cycle.
T2
Next-cycle journey
A segment confirmed after sufficient evidence moves the customer into the appropriate lifecycle journey on the next scheduled cycle - deliberate, not reactive.
T3
Track only
Minor, unconfirmed fluctuations are logged for trend purposes but generate no customer-facing communication at all - watched, not acted on.

A simple cooldown on how often a customer can be pulled into a newly triggered journey keeps the engine from causing the exact message fatigue it was built to prevent.

08
Test · Recommendations
Signals Worth Adding

A segmentation like this works with whatever data is available, but three kinds of signal usually add the most value:

Timing signals
The gap between a customer's first and second interaction, and how that gap trends. Two customers can look identical after two interactions today, but returning after two days versus two months represents a completely different trajectory - and there's currently no clock on that.
Service friction
Cancellations, unfulfilled requests, and exposure to poor service conditions. Without this, low demand and failed demand look identical - there's no way to tell whether a customer left because they didn't want to come back, or because the service failed them first.
Customer intent
Signals from before the very first completed interaction - conversion from quote to booking, searches, sessions. This would allow segmentation to begin before any behavioural history exists at all.

Priority order matters here. Routine formation is often the strongest predictor of long-term value, but it only becomes measurable after several interactions. Timing and friction signals are what let CRM catch a high-risk customer several interactions earlier - before the window to intervene has closed.

Process
The Repeatable Process Behind It

The ride-hailing details above are one application. The underlying approach is the part that transfers - design thinking applied to a data problem, run as a loop rather than a line. The same sequence carries to any behavioural-segmentation-for-lifecycle problem, whatever the dataset:

1
Frame
Define the one decision
Pin the segmentation to a single question CRM has to answer - what treatment does this customer get, and from when - before touching a variable.
2
Data understanding
Interrogate before modelling
Separate structural nulls from missing data, catch impossible values, distrust the mean where whales exist, and keep outcome fields out of the predictor set.
3
Differentiation
Isolate the real value drivers
Compare long-term vs. one-off customers to find what actually predicts value - and discard the intuitive signals that don't (often: satisfaction, timing).
4
Feature selection & ranking
Keep only admissible inputs, then rank them
Filter to behavioural, actionable, leak-free variables; use a shallow tree to order them by importance - not to set thresholds.
5
Modelling
Build an ordered rule hierarchy
Turn the ranking into first-match-wins IF/THEN rules: one customer, one segment, one readable reason a stakeholder can challenge.
6
Validation
Test against outcomes and a holdout
Cross-check segment differences against real value/maturity, and confirm the rules generalise on unseen new customers rather than overfitting.
7
Coverage
Solve cold start
Give brand-new customers a provisional tier from signup signals, then replace it with real evidence as behaviour arrives - so no one waits for treatment.
8
Activation & monitoring
Map to lifecycle actions, then self-correct
Attach each segment to one treatment and one "don't", recalculate after every interaction, and react at different speeds - promote fast, demote slow.
Loops back to step 2 continuously: every new interaction is fresh evidence.

Across all eight steps the same five design-thinking moves recur: empathise with the operator and the customer, define the one decision, ideate and eliminate, prototype the rules, then test and iterate.

09
Reflect · Synthesis
Closing Thought

Nothing in this framework requires a more sophisticated model. The hard part - and the real value - is resisting the more elegant-looking options (clustering, personas, a black-box classifier) in favour of a segmentation a CRM stakeholder can read, trust and act on immediately, with a clear answer to "why is this customer in this segment" every single time.

  • → An ordered, explainable rule beats a more accurate but opaque model, when the output has to survive a conversation with the person acting on it.
  • → Cold start isn't an edge case to patch later - for a front-loaded base, it's the majority case, and it has to be designed for from day one.
  • → Reacting at different speeds to different kinds of evidence is what keeps an automated system from undermining its own trustworthiness through noise.

The best segment isn't the one that best describes a customer today. It's the one that tells CRM exactly what to do about them - and keeps telling the truth as more evidence comes in.

A note on the examples

This article is a framework, not a report on a specific dataset. It draws on my experience in CRM and lifecycle analytics; the ride-hailing setting is a running example to make the ideas concrete.

The findings and segments described are illustrative patterns to test, not measured results, and the value-share chart is deliberately schematic. Your own data may tell a different story - which is exactly why the framework starts by checking it.
